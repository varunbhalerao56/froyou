Froyou, end to end

You say something. The app writes it down, works out what it was about, and notices when you keep coming back to the same thing — even when you never use the same words twice. Every bit of that happens on the phone.

Written for someone who hasn't seen the code. Names in this font are real things you can search for.

The one-minute version

Your words are saved immediately. Everything clever happens afterwards, in the background, and if any of it fails you still have your words. That split is the single most important design rule in the app.

What happens when you save a log

1

Your words get written to disk. That's it.

JournalRepository.save() stores the raw text and returns straight away. No AI, no waiting, nothing that can fail.

You: "Work has been okay, but I've been waiting for replies from the Malaysian team. I do feel a bit anxious." ✓ saved — visible in your log list instantly
↓
2

A mood score

Apple's NLTagger reads the text and returns a number from −1 (bleak) to +1 (good). That's the little coloured dot on the card.

mood −0.31 → the dot turns red-ish
↓
3

Keywords, from the language model

Apple's on-device language model reads the entry and names what it was about. This is not word-counting — it can say "shame" about an entry that never uses the word.

keywords: work, anxiety, replies

KeywordNamer → GenAiService.entryKeywords → Swift GenAiChannel

↓
4

The entry is cut into sentences, and each becomes a list of numbers

Apple's NLContextualEmbedding turns each sentence into 512 numbers — an "embedding". Sentences that mean similar things end up with similar numbers, even with no words in common. This is the trick that makes the whole app work.

"I've been waiting for replies" → [0.031, −0.118, 0.204, … ] (512 of them) "Still nothing back from them" → [0.029, −0.121, 0.199, … ] ← nearly identical
↓
5

Sentences get grouped into themes

Similar sentences are gathered into a ThemeCluster. This is the hard part and it has its own section below.

↓
6

Each theme gets a name

The language model reads the six most representative sentences from a theme and names it. It sees all themes together, so the names distinguish each other rather than all landing on the same word.

"waiting on work" ← from 6 sentences across 4 different entries

The hard part: grouping by meaning

You'd think you could just measure how similar two sentences' numbers are and group the close ones. That's what we tried. It failed completely, and how it failed is the most interesting thing in this codebase.

Attempt 1 — measure similarity directly

The standard measure is cosine similarity: 1.0 means identical direction, 0 means unrelated. Here's what real entries scored against a theme the app had named "anxiety":

cooking Maggi0.898
an audiobook0.933
my girlfriend0.924

Cooking, a book and a relationship — all ~0.9 "similar" to anxiety. Everything landed in one theme.

The cause has a name: anisotropy. These embeddings all share one big common direction — roughly "this is a sentence in English" — and it drowns out the part that's actually about cooking. Any two English sentences start at ~0.9 before content is even considered. There is no cutoff that works.

The fix — subtract what everything has in common

Average every sentence you've ever written into one vector. That average is the shared direction. Subtract it from each sentence, and what survives is only the distinctive part.

before: every sentence points mostly the same way → all ~0.9 similar subtract the average after: unrelated sentences point in random directions → ~0.0 similar related ones still point together → 0.3–0.6 similar

Same data, after centring

unrelated (median)−0.036
related (p99)0.513

Now there's a real gap to cut in.

Attempt 2, 3, 4 — where do you put the line?

Three sensible-sounding answers, all wrong:

CutoffReasoningWhat happened
0.55 Worked on the built-in test data 26 sentences → 25 separate themes
0.177 4× the mathematical noise floor An audiobook filed under "work"
top 10% "Assume a tenth of pairs are related" In the test data it's a fifth — broke it

The answer — Otsu's method

Stop choosing. Measure every pair of sentences and look at the shape of the results. They form two humps: a big one near zero (sentences that merely share a language) and a smaller one further out (sentences that share a subject). The cutoff belongs in the valley between them.

Otsu's method — borrowed from image processing, where it decides which pixels are ink and which are paper — finds that valley automatically. It tries every possible cut and keeps the one that makes the two groups most different from each other.

all pair scores, sorted: −0.4 −0.3 −0.28 −0.16 −0.09 │ 0.81 0.82 0.83 0.85 0.847 └──────── unrelated ────────┘ ↑ └──── related ────┘ cut here: 0.333 result: exactly 13 of 66 pairs join — the exact 13 that share a topic

Nothing is assumed about where the line sits or how many pairs fall either side. It re-measures on every rebuild, so it adapts to how repetitive your writing happens to be. One guard remains: a noise floor of 4 ÷ √512 ≈ 0.177, below which a score is indistinguishable from two random sentences — so a genuinely varied journal can't collapse into a single theme.

One more trap — order dependence

Sentences arrive one at a time, but the average needs lots of text to estimate. So the first few are grouped using the measure that doesn't work — and nothing goes back to check. Worse, a theme's "centre" drifts as it absorbs sentences, until it sits in the middle of everything and attracts everything.

So the app rebuilds all themes from scratch, oldest first, rather than only adding new sentences to old groups. reclusterAll(). It doesn't run on every save though — only once per launch, and then when the journal grows by half again or the average actually shifts. maybeRecluster() makes that call.

Where things are stored — ObjectBox

ObjectBox is the on-device database. Three kinds of object, and a handful of methods do all the work.

ObjectHolds
JournalEntryyour raw text, keywords, mood score, timestamp
JournalSentenceone sentence, its 512 numbers, which theme it belongs to
ThemeClustera theme's name, member count, and its running centre

The methods, and why each one

CallWhat it does
box.put(o)Insert or update, returning the new id. Used synchronously when saving a log — the async variant never completes under a test's fake clock.
box.putMany(list)Writes a batch in one go. The theme rebuild uses it so hundreds of sentences cost one write.
box.getAll()Everything of one kind. Deliberately rare on sentences — that's thousands of 512-number vectors.
box.removeAll()Clears a box. The rebuild throws away every theme before regrouping.
box.query(…).build().find()Filtered reads — "sentences in this theme", "entries since Monday".
store.runInTransaction(…)All-or-nothing. The rebuild deletes every theme and reassigns every sentence inside one, so a crash halfway can't leave orphans.
sentence.entry.targetA relation — a link to the owning entry. Reading its id before the object is saved throws, which cost a debugging round once.

A small trick worth knowing

Every ThemeCluster keeps sumVector — the running total of its members' numbers. So the average across the entire journal is just the sum of a few dozen themes' totals, rather than loading thousands of sentences. That one change removed the most expensive step in the app.

Vector search, and why it's unused

ObjectBox has a built-in nearest-neighbour index (nearestNeighborsF32) and the app used it at first. It can only rank raw vectors — the exact measure we established doesn't work here — so scoring moved into plain Dart. With only tens of themes, checking them all costs nothing.

Searching your journal

Two searches at once, because each fails where the other works.

you search: "sleep" literal "I only got four hours of sleep" ← the word is there meaning "I couldn't switch off last night" ← never says sleep

Literal matching misses the second. Meaning-matching misses names and places, which carry almost no semantic weight. So both run, literal results first — if the word is actually there, that's the more certain answer. Meaning matches are labelled similar in meaning in the UI, because otherwise a result that doesn't contain your search term looks like a bug. JournalSearch.

The follow-up question

If yesterday averaged rough and you haven't written today, the prompt on the home screen changes from "How are you feeling?" to something written for you. The model gets your recent theme names and a short excerpt.

themes: waiting on work · unsettled sleep excerpt: "I do feel a bit anxious about some parts." ↓ "Is that still sitting with you this morning?"

It only ever appears the day after — asking while you're still in it is nagging, not reflection. It clears the moment you write. And it's judged on the day's average, so one grumble in an otherwise fine day doesn't summon a check-in. FollowUpService. Settings → Developer → Preview follow-up question shows the whole exchange on demand.

When the model isn't there

Apple's language model needs recent hardware and Apple Intelligence switched on. It's often simply absent, so nothing may depend on it.

FeatureWithout the model
Your wordsUnaffected — never touched it
Mood dotUnaffected — different Apple framework
Grouping into themesUnaffected — that's embeddings, not the model
KeywordsFalls back to word frequency
Theme namesFalls back to a statistical method
Follow-up questionSimply doesn't appear

During development kModelOnlyLabels turns those fallbacks off, so blank means "the model said nothing" rather than something quietly filling in.

Nothing leaves the phone

Transcription, mood, embeddings, grouping and naming are all Apple frameworks running locally. There is no server, no account, and no network call anywhere in the app. That's a product promise, not a detail of the current build.
